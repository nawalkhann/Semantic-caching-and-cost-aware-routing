Cost-Aware LLM Routing: Send Easy Questions to Small Models

By Nawal · 2026-10-21 · 6 min read

Most questions a RAG product receives are lookups: "What is the API rate limit on the Pro plan?" A small model reads the retrieved paragraph and answers correctly. The large model also answers correctly, at about 25× the price per token and several times the latency. A router's job is to tell the two kinds of question apart before paying for either.

What makes a question hard

In a product-docs workload, hard questions share a few traits:

  • Several entities: "Pro vs Business storage?" needs two facts and a comparison.
  • Constraints: "We are 40 people and need SSO. What is the cheapest plan?" needs filtering and ranking.
  • Arithmetic: "What would 30 seats on Pro cost per year with annual billing?"
  • Reasoning words: why, explain, recommend, trade-offs.

Length is a poor signal on its own. "Pro vs Business storage?" is four words and hard. "Our designers keep a lot of large files around, so before we sign up I'd like to confirm how many gigabytes each seat gets on Pro" is long and easy.

A router that costs nothing to run

The router is a logistic regression over eight features that are free to compute once retrieval has run:

multi_entity   two or more entity names in the query
compare        vs, compare, more, cheapest, difference, ...
aggregate      total, annual, yearly, per year, ...
constraint     need, must, if, requires, ...
reasoning      why, explain, should, recommend, ...
numbers        count of numbers in the query (capped)
log_len        log of query length
multi_doc      retrieved docs scoring close to the top one

The score is a probability that the question is hard. At or above a threshold (default 0.5), the question goes to the large model; below it, to the small one. The default weights are set by hand. With labelled traffic, ComplexityRouter.fit() re-fits them in milliseconds. On our workload, the default router sends 98% of requests to the right tier.

Routing alone cuts cost 32% for a 1-point accuracy cost. It barely moves p95 latency (1%%), and that is expected. The slowest requests are long answers to hard questions, and a good router keeps sending those to the large model. Tail latency only falls once the cache removes repeat hard questions entirely. That's why the two techniques complement each other: together they cut cost 83% and p95 33%.

Choosing the threshold

The threshold is a business decision, so expose it as configuration. In our router sweep, raising the threshold from 0.5 to 0.8 saves another 14% of cost but loses about 4 accuracy points, because hard questions start landing on the small model. Lowering it below 0.5 spends more for no measurable gain.

When to use something heavier

  • Cascades: try the small model first and escalate when it is unsure. This works when you can trust a confidence signal, but you pay for two calls on every escalation.
  • Learned routers: a small classifier on query embeddings. Worth it once you have thousands of labelled examples and a wide variety of question types.
  • Per-tenant thresholds: premium customers get a lower threshold, so more of their traffic goes to the large model.

Start with the cheap feature router. Log the complexity score for every request; this project's dashboard does. That gives you the data to tune it and, later, to train something better.
